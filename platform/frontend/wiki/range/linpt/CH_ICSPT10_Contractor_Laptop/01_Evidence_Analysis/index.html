    Exercise ICSPT-2.2: The Contractor Laptop

    Engagement Status

    Client Masa Foods, Inc. (fictional)
    Role OT Security Engineer, Plant 1
    Phase Discovery Enumeration Exploitation (independent) Reporting

    What you know so far: You have crossed the corporate-to-OT boundary once, in Exercise 2.1, by exploiting LFI and pivoting via SSH. Exercise 2.2 is the forensic flip side: a contractor reached the OT side through the front door, and the operations switch SPAN port captured every packet of his overnight session. Your job is to read the capture and quantify what happened.

    Environment Checklist

    Every ICSPT exercise assumes you read the Before-You-Begin section in Exercise 1.1 (Touring Masa Foods) first. If you have not, open that page now: it explains the two attacker-host options and walks you through the one-time /etc/hosts setup that lets you call every target by its friendly name.

    Exercise 2.2 introduces one new host: the seized contractor laptop at ctr-laptop.masa.local, which hosts the packet capture Ben pulled from the switch. The Plant 1 PLCs, mixer, and historian are also in scope. Add the laptop entry to /etc/hosts:

    Kali (one-time for Exercise 2.2)

    # Replace the example IP with the ctr-laptop value from the
# Exercise Network panel on the OCR track page.
sudo tee -a /etc/hosts <<'HOSTS'
10.100.42.10   ctr-laptop.masa.local
HOSTS

    Confirm name resolution and reachability:

    Kali

    getent hosts ctr-laptop.masa.local plc1.masa.local
nc -zv ctr-laptop.masa.local 22

    Expected: two name-to-IP lines and succeeded! on SSH/22 for the contractor laptop. Fix connectivity before continuing if either fails.

    Scenario

    Ben Ortiz, the Plant 1 controls technician, arrived at 06:30 to start his shift and found a contractor's laptop still plugged into switch port 17 in the operations closet. The contractor, Dale Kowalski from Pulso Integration LLC, was supposed to run a read-only assessment overnight. The operations switch mirrors port 17 to a monitoring port, so Ben already has a full packet capture of Dale's session waiting on the seized laptop. Ben wants to know whether Dale stayed read-only, and Reyna Alvarado wants it in writing before the quarterly review.

    Ben handed you the laptop still running. You have SSH access as the contractor user, and the capture file contractor_session.pcap sits in the contractor home directory:

    Credentials in scope Username Password Where it is used
    Contractor laptop contractor ctr_temp_2026 SSH into the seized laptop to retrieve the capture
    Plant 1 HMI plant_op masa_op_2025 Reference only; not used in this exercise

    Why this matters

    Two numbers drive the entire incident response. The count of Modbus read operations and the count of Modbus write operations in the capture are the triage data that decide which playbook Masa activates, and that decision has legal, contractual, and safety implications:

    • Zero writes, low reads. The incident is a "contractor left laptop plugged in overnight" policy violation. Reyna writes a memo to Pulso Integration reminding them to sign out after hours, Ben disables the operations switch port with an idle timer, and the quarterly report notes the vendor management gap. No PLC state was changed, no process integrity question, no safety review. Total handling cost: one afternoon.
    • A handful of writes inside a documented change window. The incident is a scope violation. Dale tested writability without a signed change ticket, which violates the vendor access agreement. Reyna opens a conversation with Pulso Integration about contract compliance and asks for a root-cause writeup. The plant runs a sanity check on the oven setpoint register but the historian shows everything returned to baseline, so no physical restart is needed. Total handling cost: a week of paperwork.
    • Dozens of writes, or writes on registers unrelated to the stated scope. The incident escalates to a possible active compromise. The contractor's credentials may have been stolen; the laptop may have been accessed by someone else; the operator on the wire may not be Dale at all. Reyna activates the full incident response playbook, calls the shift supervisor to verify no operational anomalies, pulls plc1 offline into maintenance mode, and engages Masa's insurance carrier because an OT incident with unauthorized writes is a reportable event. Total handling cost: weeks of investigation plus possible production downtime.

    You do not know which of the three playbooks fires until you read the capture. That is why the two numbers exist as a flag: they are the go/no-go signal that determines whether Reyna goes home at 18:00 or camps in the control room through the weekend.

    Your job is to open the capture Dale left behind, count the Modbus reads and writes, and submit them in the flag format below.

    The flag follows the format OCR{ctr_<N>reads_<M>writes_plant1} where N is the number of Modbus read operations in the capture and M is the number of write operations. You count both yourself in Wireshark.

    The Plant 1 stack (plc1.masa.local, mixer.masa.local, historian.masa.local) is also reachable from your Kali. You do not need to touch the PLCs to complete this exercise, but you can use them to corroborate the evidence if you want to practice read-only technique.

    Use only Modbus function code 3 (read holding registers), function code 1 (read coils), and function code 4 (read input registers) on the PLCs. Any write function code is out of scope.

    Objectives

    • SSH into the contractor laptop as the contractor user and retrieve contractor_session.pcap
    • Open the capture in Wireshark and isolate the Modbus traffic
    • Count every Modbus read operation and every Modbus write operation by function code
    • Corroborate the findings with the contractor's notes and assessment files
    • Compose the flag from the read count and write count

    Background: Modbus Function Codes on the Wire

    Every Modbus/TCP request carries a single-byte function code that says exactly what the client asked the PLC to do. Wireshark decodes that byte and labels it for you in the Modbus tree, so you never have to read raw hex. The split between read and write is the entire case in this exercise:

    Function code Operation Read or Write Wireshark filter
    1 Read Coils Read modbus.func_code == 1
    2 Read Discrete Inputs Read modbus.func_code == 2
    3 Read Holding Registers Read modbus.func_code == 3
    4 Read Input Registers Read modbus.func_code == 4
    5 Write Single Coil Write modbus.func_code == 5
    6 Write Single Register Write modbus.func_code == 6
    15 Write Multiple Coils Write modbus.func_code == 15
    16 Write Multiple Registers Write modbus.func_code == 16

    For Dale's overnight session the reads are Function Code 3 (Read Holding Registers) and the writes are Function Code 6 (Write Single Register) and Function Code 16 (Write Multiple Registers). The display filters you will use are therefore modbus.func_code == 3 for the reads and modbus.func_code == 6 or modbus.func_code == 16 for the writes.

    Count queries, not responses

    Every Modbus request gets a response, and the response carries the same function code as the request. If you count both directions you will double every operation. Filter to one direction (the contractor's IP as the source, or the PLC's IP as the destination) before you count, so each operation is counted once. Wireshark labels request packets Query and response packets Response in the Info column.

    Tools You Will Need

    Tool Purpose Check
    ssh, sshpass Pull the capture off the seized laptop which ssh
    Wireshark Open the .pcap, filter Modbus, and count reads and writes in the GUI wireshark --version
    tshark Scriptable alternative for counting the same packets from the shell tshark --version
    cat Read the contractor's plaintext notes and assessment files which cat

    Suggested Methodology

    You build the two counts with tools, not scripts. Stage 1 retrieves the capture. Stage 2 opens it in Wireshark and isolates Modbus. Stages 3 and 4 count the reads and the writes by display filter. Stage 5 cross-references the contractor's own notes. Stage 6 records the counts and assembles the flag.

    Stage 1: Retrieve the Capture from the Laptop

    SSH into the seized laptop and confirm the three evidence files are present, then copy the capture back to your Kali so you can open it in Wireshark locally.

    Kali

    sshpass -p 'ctr_temp_2026' ssh -o StrictHostKeyChecking=no -o UserKnownHostsFile=/dev/null contractor@ctr-laptop.masa.local 'ls -la ~'
    Expected output 

    total 36
drwxr-sr-x    1 contract contract      4096 Apr 11 23:00 .
drwxr-xr-x    1 root     root          4096 Apr 11 23:00 ..
-rw-r--r--    1 contract contract       ... Apr 11 23:00 assessment.txt
-rw-r--r--    1 contract contract       ... Apr 11 23:00 contractor_session.pcap
-rw-r--r--    1 contract contract       ... Apr 11 23:00 notes.txt
    The laptop has three files in the contractor home: the packet capture, the notes, and the draft assessment.

    Pull the capture down to your Kali with scp:

    Kali

    sshpass -p 'ctr_temp_2026' scp -o StrictHostKeyChecking=no -o UserKnownHostsFile=/dev/null contractor@ctr-laptop.masa.local:~/contractor_session.pcap /tmp/contractor_session.pcap

    Stage 2: Open the Capture and Isolate Modbus

    Open the file in the Wireshark GUI and narrow the view to the Modbus traffic.

    1. Open the capture. From a terminal run wireshark /tmp/contractor_session.pcap &, or inside Wireshark choose File > Open and select /tmp/contractor_session.pcap.
    2. Filter to Modbus. In the display filter bar (the wide bar directly under the toolbar), type modbus and press Enter. The packet list narrows to just the contractor's Modbus queries and the PLC responses.
    3. Read the function codes. Click any query row in the Packet List (top pane), then in the Packet Details (middle pane) expand the Modbus node. The Function Code field names the operation in plain text (for example Function Code: 3, Read Holding Registers). Walk a few packets so you recognize the read and write labels on sight.

    Stage 3: Count the Read Operations

    Set the display filter to the read function code, restrict it to the contractor's queries so responses are not double-counted, and read the count off the status bar.

    1. In the display filter bar type modbus.func_code == 3 and press Enter. To count only the contractor's queries, append his source address, for example modbus.func_code == 3 && ip.src == <contractor-ip> (read the contractor IP from the source column of any query packet).
    2. Read the count from the status bar at the bottom right of the window. The Displayed number is the read operation count.
    3. As a cross-check, open Statistics > Conversations, select the TCP tab, and confirm the contractor-to-PLC conversations on port 502 line up with what the filter showed.

    (tshark option)

    You can produce the same read count from the shell: 

    tshark -r /tmp/contractor_session.pcap -Y 'modbus.func_code == 3 && modbus.request_frame' 2>/dev/null | wc -l
    Filtering on modbus.request_frame keeps responses out of the count automatically, so each read is counted once.

    Stage 4: Count the Write Operations

    Repeat the count with the write function codes.

    1. In the display filter bar type modbus.func_code == 6 or modbus.func_code == 16 and press Enter. Restrict to the contractor's queries the same way if you want, for example (modbus.func_code == 6 or modbus.func_code == 16) && ip.src == <contractor-ip>.
    2. Read the Displayed count from the status bar. That is the write operation count.
    3. Click each write query and expand the Modbus tree to confirm the function code and the register address. Note which registers Dale wrote to; you will corroborate them against his notes in Stage 5.

    (tshark option)

    The shell equivalent for the write count: 

    tshark -r /tmp/contractor_session.pcap -Y '(modbus.func_code == 6 || modbus.func_code == 16) && modbus.request_frame' 2>/dev/null | wc -l

    Stage 5: Cross-Reference the Notes

    Confirm your write count against Dale's own words. The notes and assessment are plaintext on the laptop.

    Kali

    sshpass -p 'ctr_temp_2026' ssh -o StrictHostKeyChecking=no -o UserKnownHostsFile=/dev/null contractor@ctr-laptop.masa.local 'cat notes.txt; echo ---; cat assessment.txt'
    Expected output (trimmed) 

    ...
I ended up bumping the oven setpoint a couple of times (up then back to
baseline) to verify the register was writable. I know that technically
counts as a write on an OT network. I will log it in the assessment and
call Reyna first thing in the morning.
---
Writes performed against plc1:
- Oven setpoint bumped and returned to baseline.
...
    Dale's own notes describe the oven-setpoint writes he performed. Match the registers named in the notes to the write queries you saw in Stage 4. If the capture shows more writes than the notes admit to, that gap is itself a finding worth flagging to Reyna.

    Stage 6: Record Your Findings and Compose the Flag

    Fill in the table from your own Wireshark counts, then assemble the flag from the template. Substitute the read count for <N> and the write count for <M>.

    Evidence summary:

    Question Your value
    Read operations in the capture (modbus.func_code == 3) <N>
    Write operations in the capture (modbus.func_code == 6 or 16) <M>
    Registers Dale wrote to (from the Modbus tree)
    Writes Dale documented in notes.txt / assessment.txt

    Flag template: OCR{ctr_<N>reads_<M>writes_plant1}

    Replace <N> with your read count and <M> with your write count, then submit the assembled string in the OCR platform flag box. The platform tells you immediately whether the counts are correct, so the finished flag is not printed here.

    Output Interpretation

    • The Modbus response carries the same function code as the request, so a naive modbus.func_code == 3 filter counts both the query and the reply. Restricting to the request direction (modbus.request_frame, or the contractor IP as source) is what makes the count match the number of operations Dale actually performed
    • Dale wrote the classic "contractor with read-only scope who ended up writing a little" story into his own notes. The pattern is a very common finding in real engagements. Contractors test writability "just to make sure" and the write is never approved in advance
    • The "bumped and returned to baseline" language in the assessment is the piece a quality-of-evidence review would flag: Dale returned the setpoint to the baseline, which is the right instinct, but he still issued write queries against an OT device without documented authorization

    Analysis Questions

    1. Why is a SPAN-port packet capture so valuable during an incident investigation?

    Reveal answer

    A capture records the exact bytes that crossed the wire, in order, with timestamps, independent of anything the host claims it did. Unlike bash history or application logs, which a careful attacker can clear or forge, a switch-mirrored capture sits outside the attacker's reach. For OT protocols like Modbus it is decisive because the protocol is unauthenticated and unencrypted: every function code, register address, and value is in plaintext on the wire, so the capture tells you exactly what was read and what was written without trusting the operator's account of events.

    2. Dale's capture shows a Write Single Register to the oven setpoint followed immediately by a second write returning it to baseline. What does that pattern tell you about his intent?

    Reveal answer

    It tells you that Dale was testing writability, not trying to sabotage the plant. He wrote a new value, presumably read it back to confirm the write succeeded, then wrote the baseline value back immediately. That is the "test, verify, revert" pattern that contractors use to prove a register is writable without leaving the process in a modified state. The problem is not intent; the problem is that he did it without prior documented approval and without a maintenance window from operations.

    3. The flag format requires both counts. What would a defender do differently if the capture showed reads and zero writes?

    Reveal answer

    The incident closes with a note that the read-only scope was honored. The laptop still sits overnight on an OT segment, which is its own policy violation, but the integrity of the PLC is intact and no maintenance-window notification is required. The writes are what turns the incident from "contractor left laptop plugged in" into "contractor wrote to a PLC outside of a maintenance window."

    4. How would you monitor for a repeat of this incident in production?

    Reveal answer

    Three layers: a Modbus-aware IDS that alerts on any write function code from a source outside the HMI subnet (you drafted one of these in Exercise 3.4); a process to disable idle operations switch ports after a fixed period; and a vendor-access policy that requires contractor laptops to use a checked-out plant-owned device with a supervised network connection during the maintenance window only. None of these are free, but all three are cheaper than reviewing incident captures after the fact.

    Defensive Recommendations to Masa

    Finding Risk Mitigation
    Contractor laptop left plugged into operations VLAN overnight Critical Disable operations switch ports after 30 minutes of no activity, require sign-out on the vendor management log
    Contractor wrote to plc1 outside of a documented maintenance window High Deploy a Modbus-aware IDS with write alerting from non-HMI sources; require vendor write operations to be ticketed in advance
    No always-on packet capture on the operations VLAN Low Keep a rolling SPAN-port capture so every OT session is reconstructable after the fact, the way this incident was
    No vendor access policy differentiates "scope: read" from "scope: read and test writability" Medium Update vendor access templates so "test writability" requires an explicit change ticket

    Key Takeaways

    • A SPAN-port packet capture is the highest-fidelity forensic artifact on an OT segment because Modbus is plaintext and the capture sits outside the attacker's reach
    • Wireshark decodes the Modbus function code and labels read versus write for you; the modbus.func_code display filter plus the status bar count is the cheapest way to tally operations
    • Always filter to the request direction (modbus.request_frame) before counting, or the query-and-response pairing doubles every operation
    • Contractors almost always "just test" writability even when they are scoped read-only. Plan your defenses around that reality
    • The flag format (explicit counts in the flag string) forces you to do the counting yourself in the analyzer, which is the skill the exercise is actually teaching
