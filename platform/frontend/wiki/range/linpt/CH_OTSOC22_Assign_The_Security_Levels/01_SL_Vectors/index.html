    Exercise OTSOC-6.2: Assign the Security Levels

    Scenario

    You drew the zones in the previous exercise. Now you put a number on each one. The insurer's questionnaire does not stop at a partition: it asks what protection each zone is supposed to deliver, and IEC 62443-3-3 answers that question with a security-level target. A security-level target (SL-T) is not a single grade for a zone. It is a vector of seven values, one per Foundational Requirement, set by the worst-case consequence of compromising that zone.

    Your job in this exercise is to assign a per-FR SL-T vector to every zone you defined, drive each value from the consequence model, then find the one place where the target you set exceeds what the plant can actually deliver. That gap lives on the IT-to-OT boundary conduit, where the flat network delivers no real protection while the target demands SL 2 or higher. You name the compensating control that closes it, then POST the SL vectors and the gap analysis to the security-levels rubric, which releases the flag once the per-FR vectors, the consequence ranking, and the SL-T versus SL-C gap are all present.

    Why this matters

    An SL-T vector is how 62443 turns "this zone is important" into something an engineer can procure and an insurer can audit. IEC 62443-3-3 defines four security levels: SL 1 protects against casual or coincidental violation, SL 2 against intentional violation using simple means, SL 3 against sophisticated means with moderate resources, and SL 4 against sophisticated means with extended resources. Assigning a vector forces you to reason per requirement: a zone might need strong authentication (FR1) and integrity (FR3) but tolerate weak confidentiality (FR4), and a single overall grade would hide that.

    The gap analysis is where the deliverable earns its keep. SL-T is the target. SL-C is the capability the installed equipment and current architecture can actually achieve. When SL-T exceeds SL-C you have three choices: buy better equipment, change the architecture, or add a compensating control. On a flat creamery network the IT-to-OT boundary has an SL-C near zero and an SL-T of 2 or higher, and the compensating control that closes the gap is the industrial DMZ you design next. Naming the gap and its control is the language an underwriter and a plant manager both understand.

    • Submit the SL vectors and gap analysis to the security-levels rubric and retrieve the flag

    Background: The Seven Foundational Requirements

    IEC 62443-3-3 organizes every security capability into seven Foundational Requirements. An SL-T vector assigns one security level to each.

    FR Name What it covers
    FR1 Identification and Authentication Control (IAC) Who and what is allowed to connect
    FR2 Use Control (UC) What an authenticated identity is permitted to do
    FR3 System Integrity (SI) Protection of code, configuration, and data from tampering
    FR4 Data Confidentiality (DC) Protection of information from disclosure
    FR5 Restricted Data Flow (RDF) Segmentation and conduit enforcement between zones
    FR6 Timely Response to Events (TRE) Logging, alerting, and the ability to react
    FR7 Resource Availability (RA) Keeping the process running under stress or attack

    Why a vector and not a single number

    A safety zone may demand SL 3 on authentication (FR1) and integrity (FR3) because a defeated trip kills people, yet only SL 1 on confidentiality (FR4) because the trip setpoints are not secret. A single overall SL would force you to either over-build confidentiality or under-build integrity. The vector lets each requirement carry the level its consequence actually warrants.

    Walkthrough

    Step 1: Map consequence to a per-FR vector

    Carry forward the consequence ranking from Exercise 6.1. The higher a zone's worst-case harm, the higher its SL-T on the requirements that defend against that harm. Reason one zone at a time. The ammonia safety zone is the worst case: a defeated trip is a toxic release. An attacker reaching the SIS must be stopped at authentication (FR1), the trip setpoints must be tamper-proof (FR3), and the SIS must be unreachable from the rest of the plant (FR5). Those three earn SL 3. Confidentiality (FR4) is low because the setpoints are not secret.

    Work the same logic down the consequence ranking and you get the target table below. The numbers are defensible targets, not the only correct answer; what the grader checks is that the safety and food-safety zones outrank the telemetry zone and that you justify each value.

    Zone FR1 IAC FR2 UC FR3 SI FR4 DC FR5 RDF FR6 TRE FR7 RA Driver
    Safety (ammonia SIS) 3 3 3 1 3 3 3 Defeated trip is a worker-safety event
    Pasteurizer (food safety) 3 3 3 1 3 2 2 Under-processed milk shipped, public-health event
    Control (mix, freeze, ammonia BPCS) 2 2 2 1 2 2 3 Lost batch, process upset
    Cold-storage telemetry 2 1 2 1 2 2 2 Spoilage if telemetry goes stale, lower consequence
    Supervisory (opcua, historian) 2 2 2 2 2 2 2 Loss of view, degraded monitoring
    IT-to-OT boundary conduit 3 3 3 2 3 3 2 Sole path from IT to every control zone

    The safety zone and the pasteurizer zone sit at the top, the cold-storage telemetry zone sits lower, and the IT-to-OT conduit carries a high target because it is the one path that touches everything.

    Step 2: Justify the safety-zone vector per FR

    The grader wants the per-FR reasoning written down, not just the numbers. Spell out why the safety zone earns SL 3 on the requirements that matter. FR1 at SL 3 means no casual or simple-means actor can authenticate to the SIS; only a sophisticated attacker with moderate resources should even reach the authentication boundary. FR3 at SL 3 means the trip setpoints cannot be altered without detection, which directly answers the attack where 40010 and 40011 are weakened. FR5 at SL 3 means the SIS sits behind a conduit that blocks all but the status path, so a compromise of the ammonia BPCS cannot pivot into the safety zone.

    Step 3: Find the SL-T versus SL-C gap on the boundary

    Now compare target against capability on the IT-to-OT boundary conduit. The target you assigned is SL 3 on FR5 (restricted data flow). The capability the current plant delivers is the flat network: no firewall, no ACL, no conduit, so the SL-C on FR5 is effectively SL 0. The gap is the distance between them.

    Conduit FR5 SL-T (target) FR5 SL-C (current) Gap
    IT-to-OT boundary 3 0 3 levels, target far exceeds capability

    A gap this wide is exactly what the insurer is looking for: it quantifies how exposed the plant is today. The corpweb LFI from the next exercise is the proof that SL-C is near zero, since an internet-facing portal bug reaches a control-network credential with nothing in the way.

    Step 4: Name the compensating control

    When SL-T exceeds SL-C you close the gap by buying equipment, changing the architecture, or adding a compensating control. Here the answer is architectural: an industrial DMZ at Level 3.5 with a deny-by-default boundary firewall and a brokered jump host. Map the control to the requirements it satisfies, so the underwriter can trace the fix back to the gap.

    Compensating control FR raised How it closes the gap
    Industrial DMZ with deny-by-default firewall FR5 RDF Replaces the flat segment with an enforced conduit
    Brokered jump host with MFA FR1 IAC, FR2 UC No direct IT credential reaches the control zone
    Conduit logging and alerting FR6 TRE Boundary crossings become visible and auditable

    Step 5: Write and submit the deliverable

    Read the required elements first so your text names every keyword the grader checks: the SL-T vectors, the per-FR justification, the consequence ranking, and the SL-T versus SL-C gap.

    Kali - read the required elements for the security-levels deliverable

    curl -s "http://$RUBRIC/rubric?deliverable=security-levels" | python3 -m json.tool

    Compose the deliverable in a file so you can iterate. Cover the vectors, the ranking, the gap, and the compensating control in one submission.

    Kali - write the SL-vector deliverable to a file

    cat > /tmp/security-levels.txt <<'EOF'
Pecan Creek Creamery security level target assignment per IEC 62443-3-3.
For each zone and conduit in the partition a per-FR SL-T vector is assigned by
worst-case consequence, one value per foundational requirement FR1 through FR7.
Safety zone: the ammonia SIS safety instrumented system is ranked highest on
FR1, FR3, and FR5 because a defeated trip is a worker-safety event; each value
is justified per FR. Pasteurizer food-safety zone ranks next. The cold-storage
telemetry zone is lower consequence with a lower SL-T security level target.
Consequence ranking: safety and food safety above telemetry.
Boundary gap: on the IT-to-OT conduit the target SL-T exceeds the current
capability SL-C, a clear capability gap where target exceeds capability, because
the flat network delivers no restricted data flow. The compensating control is
an industrial dmz (iDMZ) with a deny by default boundary firewall and ACL and a
brokered jump host, mapped to FR1, FR2, FR5, and FR6.
EOF
wc -l /tmp/security-levels.txt

    POST the file with deliverable set to security-levels and read the flag field.

    Kali - POST the deliverable to the security-levels rubric

    jq -n --rawfile s /tmp/security-levels.txt \
   '{deliverable:"security-levels", submission:$s}' \
  | curl -s -X POST "http://$RUBRIC/check" \
      -H 'Content-Type: application/json' -d @- \
  | python3 -m json.tool

    Expected output (flag value masked)

    {
    "deliverable": "security-levels",
    "score": 1.0,
    "passed": true,
    "flag": "OCR{pcc_sl_vectors_<masked>}"
}

    A passed of true means the vectors, the ranking, and the gap analysis were all present. If passed is false, the response tells you which element is missing; add it to the file and POST again until it passes.

    Output Interpretation

    • A single overall SL hides the per-FR trade-off; the grader and the standard both expect a vector, so a submission that gives one number per zone will read as incomplete
    • The consequence ranking is load-bearing: if the cold-storage zone outranks the safety zone in your text, the deliverable contradicts the partition and the score drops
    • The SL-T versus SL-C gap is the element students miss most; the grader checks for the words target, capability, and gap, because that triplet is what an insurer audits
    • The compensating control must map to the requirements it raises (FR1, FR2, FR5, FR6), otherwise it reads as a vague recommendation rather than a closed gap

    Analysis Questions

    1. Why is an SL-T a vector of seven values instead of a single security level for the zone?

    Reveal answer

    Different Foundational Requirements carry different consequences in the same zone. A safety zone needs SL 3 authentication and integrity because a defeated trip kills people, but only SL 1 confidentiality because the setpoints are not secret. A single number would force over-building one requirement or under-building another. The vector lets each requirement carry the level its own consequence warrants, which is also how equipment is actually certified.

    2. What is the difference between SL-T and SL-C, and what do you do when SL-T exceeds SL-C?

    Reveal answer

    SL-T is the target you assign from consequence. SL-C is the capability the installed equipment and current architecture can actually achieve. When the target exceeds the capability you have a gap, and you close it by procuring better equipment, changing the architecture, or adding a compensating control. On the flat creamery network the IT-to-OT conduit has an SL-C near zero against an SL-T of 3, and the compensating control is the industrial DMZ.

    3. Why does the IT-to-OT boundary conduit carry a higher SL-T than most of the individual zones?

    Reveal answer

    The boundary conduit is the single path through which IT traffic can reach every control zone, so its compromise inherits the consequence of everything behind it, including the safety zone. A conduit that touches the highest-consequence zone must be protected to at least that zone's level on restricted data flow and authentication. The flat network gives it none of that today, which is why the gap on this conduit is the widest in the plant.

    Capture the Flag

    The security-levels rubric grader releases the flag when your deliverable carries the per-FR SL-T vectors, the consequence ranking with safety and food safety on top, and the SL-T versus SL-C gap on the IT-to-OT boundary with its compensating control. You earn it by submitting a complete vector analysis. The flag describes what you accomplished: the SL vectors assigned across the plant.
