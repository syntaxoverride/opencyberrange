    Exercise ICSPT-6.4: Board Report

    Engagement Status

    Client Masa Foods, Inc. (fictional)
    Role OT Security Engineer, All Plants
    Phase Discovery Incident Response Capstone (board-level report) Engagement Complete

    What you know so far: Exercise 6.3 gave you a complete kill chain reconstruction of the February 14, 2025 cross-site incident. You know the initial access vector (LFI), the pivot mechanism (SSH with stolen credentials), and the per-plant impact (oven setpoint, SIS trip, phantom pallets). Exercise 6.4 is the capstone: translate your technical findings into a board-level incident report that a non-technical audience can act on. The rubric checker scores your report against executive communication standards.

    Environment Checklist

    Exercise 6.4 uses both the evidence server (for reference data) and the rubric checker (for submission scoring). Verify connectivity to each.

    Kali

    curl -s http://evidence.masa.local:8080/ | head -10
curl -s http://rubric.masa.local:8080/rubric | head -30

    Both servers should respond. The evidence server provides the incident artifacts you analyzed in Exercise 6.3. The rubric server scores your board report.

    Scenario

    The Masa Foods board of directors has called an emergency session following the February 14 incident. Reyna Alvarado has been asked to present a written incident report suitable for board members who have no technical background. The report must explain what happened, quantify the business impact, attribute the attack using industry-standard frameworks, and propose prioritized remediation. Reyna has delegated the writing to you. A rubric checker at rubric.masa.local:8080 scores your report against the board's expectations.

    Why this matters

    Most cybersecurity professionals can write a technical incident report. Very few can write a board report. The difference is audience: board members do not understand Modbus function codes, S7 protection levels, or CIP service bytes. Board members understand production downtime, food safety risk, regulatory exposure, and dollars. A board report that says "the attacker sent Modbus FC 6 with value 500 to DB1 offset 0" is useless to a director. A board report that says "the attacker remotely raised an industrial oven to 500 degrees F, exceeding the safe operating range and creating a fire and food safety hazard" is actionable.

    The professional skill Exercise 6.4 builds is the ability to translate deep technical knowledge into business language without losing accuracy. Every sentence in the report must be technically correct, but no sentence should require ICS protocol knowledge to understand. Board reports are the deliverable that justifies security budgets, and security budgets are the mechanism that funds the controls you recommended in Exercises 6.1 and 6.2.

    What makes a good board report

    A board-level incident report has several characteristics that distinguish it from a technical report:

    1. Non-technical language throughout. Replace protocol names with plain descriptions. "Modbus Function Code 6" becomes "a remote command to change the oven temperature." "S7comm Write Var" becomes "a remote command to increase chemical dosing."
    2. Business impact focus. Quantify downtime in hours, food safety risk in regulatory terms (FDA, FSMA), and financial exposure in dollars or production batches lost.
    3. Timeline with cause and effect. Board members need to see the sequence: how did the attacker get in, what did they do, and what was the consequence at each step?
    4. MITRE ATT&CK ICS references. The board's external auditors and insurance carrier expect standardized attack technique references. Use technique IDs (T0855 for Unauthorized Command Message, T0836 for Modify Parameter) alongside plain-language descriptions.
    5. Prioritized recommendations. Rank remediation actions by risk reduction and cost. The board approves budgets, so recommendations must be sequenced by priority with rough cost categories (low, medium, high).

    Background: Board Communication for Cyber Incidents

    MITRE ATT&CK for ICS Techniques

    The February 14 incident maps to several MITRE ATT&CK for ICS techniques. The rubric checker expects at least two of these:

    Technique ID Name Masa Foods Mapping
    T0855 Unauthorized Command Message Attacker sent write commands to PLCs across all three plants without authorization
    T0836 Modify Parameter Attacker changed oven setpoint (Plant 1), caustic dosing rate (Plant 2), and pallet count (Plant 3)
    T0859 Valid Accounts Attacker used stolen OT credentials (plant_op) to authenticate to the jump server
    T0886 Remote Services Attacker used SSH to pivot from the corporate network to the OT jump server

    Translating technical findings to business language

    Technical Finding Board Language
    Modbus FC 6 wrote oven setpoint to 500 degrees F An unauthorized party remotely raised Plant 1 oven temperature to 500 degrees F, exceeding the safe operating limit and creating fire and food safety hazards
    S7comm Write Var set caustic dosing to 200 ml/min, SIS tripped An unauthorized party increased chemical dosing in Plant 2 water treatment to dangerous levels, triggering an automatic safety shutdown that halted production
    CIP Write Tag set PalletCount to 9999 An unauthorized party injected false inventory data into Plant 3 packaging, causing the system to report 9999 phantom pallets and disrupting shipping logistics
    LFI on corporate web server The attacker exploited a software vulnerability on a public-facing web application to steal internal credentials
    SSH pivot through jump server The attacker used stolen credentials to access the industrial control network through a remote access gateway

    Report structure the rubric expects

    Based on the rubric checker requirements, the board report should include:

    1. Executive Summary: 3 to 5 sentences covering what happened, when, and the overall business impact
    2. Incident Timeline: Chronological sequence of attacker actions from initial access to process impact
    3. Per-Plant Impact: Separate subsection for each plant describing the physical consequence in business terms
    4. Initial Access and Pivot: How the attacker entered the network and reached the OT environment
    5. MITRE ATT&CK ICS Mapping: At minimum T0855 and T0836 with plain-language explanations
    6. Remediation Recommendations: Prioritized list with cost categories
    7. Detection Improvements: Specific monitoring and alerting changes to catch similar attacks in the future
    8. Authentication and Access Control: Recommendations for credential management and access restrictions

    Suggested Methodology

    Exercise 6.4 is an independent exercise. No step-by-step walkthrough is provided. Instead, here are the stages a methodical report author would follow.

    Stage 1: Retrieve the Rubric

    Query the rubric endpoint to understand the exact scoring criteria. Each criterion maps to a section of the board report.

    Kali

    curl -s http://rubric.masa.local:8080/rubric | jq .

    Record every criterion. Your report must address all of them to pass.

    Stage 2: Gather Your Evidence

    Review the kill chain timeline you built in Exercise 6.3. Pull up the incident summary, historian data, and PCAPs as reference. You do not need to re-analyze the evidence, but you do need to translate every technical finding into business language for the board.

    Stage 3: Draft the Report

    Write the board report as a plain-text document. Follow the structure from the Background section. For each section, ask yourself: "Would a board member with no cybersecurity background understand every sentence?" If the answer is no, rewrite the sentence in plainer language.

    Key writing principles for board reports:

    • Lead with impact, not with method. "Plant 1 oven temperature was raised to dangerous levels" before "the attacker used Modbus Function Code 6."
    • Quantify wherever possible. Hours of downtime, batches of product at risk, regulatory standards potentially violated.
    • Use MITRE ATT&CK ICS technique IDs as parenthetical references, not as the primary description. Write "unauthorized remote commands (MITRE T0855)" rather than "T0855 was observed."
    • Prioritize recommendations by risk reduction. The most impactful, lowest-cost fix comes first.

    Stage 4: Submit and Iterate

    Submit the report to the rubric checker and review the feedback. The checker evaluates each required section independently, so partial credit is possible. Focus revisions on the sections that failed.

    Kali

    curl -s -X POST http://rubric.masa.local:8080/check \
  -H "Content-Type: text/plain" \
  --data-binary @board_report.txt | jq .

    Most reports require two to four iterations. Common rejection reasons include: missing MITRE technique IDs, technical jargon without plain-language explanation, missing per-plant impact sections, or recommendations without prioritization.

    Stage 5: Record the Flag

    When the rubric checker accepts the report, the response includes the flag. Record it and submit.

    Output Interpretation

    • A response with "pass": false lists every failing criterion. Address each one specifically rather than rewriting the entire report
    • The rubric checker evaluates text content, not formatting. Plain text with clear section headers is sufficient
    • If the checker rejects for "missing executive summary," ensure your report opens with a standalone paragraph that a board member could read in 30 seconds and understand the full situation
    • If the checker rejects for "missing MITRE ATT&CK ICS," verify you included at least T0855 (Unauthorized Command Message) and T0836 (Modify Parameter) with their technique IDs
    • If the checker rejects for "missing authentication recommendations," verify you addressed credential rotation, multi-factor authentication for OT access, and the elimination of shared accounts

    Analysis Questions

    1. The board report must avoid technical jargon, but it also must be technically accurate. How do you handle the tension between accessibility and precision?

    Reveal answer

    Use layered language. The primary sentence uses plain business terms ("the attacker remotely raised the oven temperature to dangerous levels"). A parenthetical or footnote provides the technical detail for the security team and auditors ("via Modbus Function Code 6, writing value 500 to the oven setpoint register"). Board members read the sentence. Auditors read the parenthetical. Both audiences are served without compromising accuracy or accessibility.

    2. The rubric requires MITRE ATT&CK for ICS technique references. Why do board reports include framework references that board members will not understand?

    Reveal answer

    Board reports are read by more than just board members. The company's cyber insurance carrier, external auditors, legal counsel, and regulatory bodies all review incident reports. These stakeholders expect standardized technique references because they use them to assess coverage, liability, and compliance. MITRE ATT&CK ICS is the industry-standard taxonomy. Including technique IDs signals professional rigor and makes the report defensible in regulatory and legal contexts. The board members themselves may skip the IDs, but the IDs serve the broader audience of the document.

    3. Your remediation recommendations must be prioritized. What criteria determine the priority order?

    Reveal answer

    Priority is determined by the intersection of risk reduction and implementation cost. The highest-priority recommendation reduces the most risk at the lowest cost. For the Masa Foods incident, patching the LFI vulnerability and rotating stolen credentials are highest priority because they close the exact attack path used, and both are low-cost actions. Deploying an IT/OT DMZ with multi-factor authentication is high priority and medium cost. Implementing a full ICS-aware monitoring platform is high priority but also high cost, so it ranks lower despite its importance. The board approves budgets sequentially, so the order determines what gets funded first.

    4. The February 14 attacker hit all three plants. Should the board report recommend separate remediation for each plant or a unified platform-wide approach?

    Reveal answer

    Both. The report should recommend immediate per-plant fixes (credential rotation, firewall rules specific to each protocol) and a unified long-term architecture (centralized IT/OT DMZ, enterprise-wide MFA, cross-plant monitoring). Per-plant fixes are fast and address the specific vulnerabilities exploited. The unified approach prevents the next attacker from finding a different path to the same targets. Board members understand the distinction between "stop the bleeding" (per-plant) and "prevent recurrence" (platform-wide), so framing recommendations in those two categories makes the prioritization intuitive.

    Defensive Recommendations to Masa

    Finding Risk Mitigation
    No board-level incident reporting process existed before this attack High Establish a documented incident reporting template that the CISO completes within 48 hours of any OT security event
    Technical incident reports are not translated for non-technical stakeholders Medium Require every incident report to include a board-ready executive summary written in business language
    MITRE ATT&CK ICS framework not previously used in Masa incident documentation Medium Adopt MITRE ATT&CK for ICS as the standard taxonomy for all future incident reports, enabling consistent communication with insurance carriers and auditors
    Remediation recommendations in past reports were not prioritized by cost and risk High Require all future remediation sections to rank actions by risk reduction and include cost categories (low, medium, high) so the board can make informed funding decisions

    Key Takeaways

    • Board reports translate technical findings into business language. Every sentence must be understandable by a reader with no cybersecurity background
    • Lead with business impact (downtime, safety risk, regulatory exposure), not with protocol details. Method is supporting evidence, not the headline
    • MITRE ATT&CK for ICS technique IDs (T0855, T0836) serve auditors, insurers, and regulators even when board members do not read them directly
    • Prioritized remediation recommendations are the actionable output of the report. The board funds fixes in the order you present them
    • Writing a board report is the final step in the OT security engagement lifecycle: discovery, exploitation, detection, defense design, incident response, and executive communication
