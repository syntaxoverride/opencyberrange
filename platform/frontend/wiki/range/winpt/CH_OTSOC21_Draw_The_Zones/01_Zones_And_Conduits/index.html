    Exercise OTSOC-6.1: Draw the Zones and Conduits

    Scenario

    Pecan Creek Creamery runs a flat OT network. Every controller, the supervisory layer, the historian, and the corporate web portal share one broadcast domain with no internal boundary. A blend-tank PLC, an ammonia safety controller, and an internet-facing portal all sit in the same Layer 2 segment, which means a foothold anywhere is a foothold everywhere.

    The insurer's OT security questionnaire has landed on the SOC desk, and the first deliverable it demands is a zone-and-conduit architecture drawn to IEC 62443-3-2. Your job in this exercise is the partition itself: group the plant assets into security zones, define the conduits that connect them, and isolate the ammonia safety instrumented system in its own safety zone. The plant is attached read-only so you can build the partition from the real asset inventory rather than from a guess. When the model is complete you POST it to the architecture rubric grader, which releases the flag once the safety zone is isolated and the required conduits are present.

    Why this matters

    IEC 62443-3-2 is the standard the insurer's questionnaire is written against, and its ZCR (Zone and Conduit Requirements) workflow is the first thing an underwriter looks for: can the plant even describe its own segmentation. A zone is a grouping of assets that share the same security requirement and the same worst-case consequence. A conduit is the controlled communication path between zones, and every conduit is a place you can put a firewall, an ACL, or a data diode.

    The single most important call in the whole partition is the ammonia safety system. A safety instrumented function that an attacker can defeat is a worker-safety event, an ammonia release, not a spoiled batch. IEC 61511 and 62443 both demand that the SIS not share a failure domain with the basic process control it protects. Putting the ammonia SIS in its own safety zone, separate from the ammonia BPCS, is the move that keeps a compromise of the refrigeration controller from also disabling the trip that protects the people on the floor. The flag is only proof of submission; the partition you draw is the artifact the insurer actually buys.

    Background: The IEC 62443-3-2 ZCR Workflow

    IEC 62443-3-2 partitions a plant in a fixed order. The first three Zone and Conduit Requirements are the ones you exercise here.

    Step Name What you produce
    ZCR1 Define the system under consideration (SUC) A boundary: which assets are in scope, which are out
    ZCR2 Initial high-level risk assessment The worst-case consequence per asset group
    ZCR3 Partition into zones and conduits The zones grouped by shared requirement, the conduits between them

    A zone groups assets that share a security requirement and a consequence. A conduit is the controlled path between two zones, and it is the only sanctioned way traffic crosses a zone boundary. The discipline of 62443 is that you justify every zone against the consequence model from ZCR2: the higher the worst-case harm, the more isolation the zone earns.

    Why the SIS gets its own zone

    A basic process control system (BPCS) runs the process. A safety instrumented system (SIS) exists only to trip the process to a safe state when the BPCS fails. If the SIS shares a network and a failure domain with the BPCS, a single compromise can disable both the control and the protection at once, which is exactly the TRITON attack pattern. IEC 62443 and IEC 61511 require the SIS to be independent, so it earns its own safety zone with the tightest conduit rules in the plant.

    Walkthrough

    Step 1: Define the system under consideration (ZCR1)

    Start by drawing the boundary. Enumerate the live plant so your partition is built from observed assets, not from the inherited spreadsheet. The plantwide historian at port 3000 serves a real operator dashboard, and its "About this historian" panel lists every source it polls with the protocol behind each one, which is the asset inventory you map into zones. Open the dashboard in your browser and read the source list off the About panel first, because seeing the assets and their protocols in one view is how a defender grounds the partition.

    Kali - open the historian dashboard in the browser

    Read the historian IP off the Exercise Network panel and open it in your browser on port 3000, for example http://HIST_IP:3000/. Scroll to the "About this historian" panel and read the polled-source inventory: each source name paired with the protocol it speaks.

    Interpret the About panel one source at a time: each row is an asset you place in a zone, and the protocol next to it tells you what kind of controller it is. The blend tank, the ammonia BPCS, and the ammonia SIS answer on Modbus, the freezer answers on CIP, and the cold-storage RTU answers on DNP3. Anything the historian polls is inside your system under consideration; the corporate portal is outside the OT boundary but touches it, so it belongs in the IT zone with a conduit into the plant.

    For analysts who want the same inventory as machine-readable JSON, the historian exposes the source list at /api/sources. Pulling it programmatically is a legitimate cross-check that the dashboard and the API agree, and it hands you a list you can paste straight into your notes.

    Kali (optional) - cross-check the source list as JSON

    # Read the historian IP off the Exercise Network panel.
HIST=10.100.42.9
curl -s "http://$HIST:3000/api/sources"

    The JSON returns the same list of control devices the About panel showed, which confirms the blend tank, the ammonia controllers, the freezer, and the cold-storage RTU are live. The API and the dashboard agreeing is your evidence the inventory is real and complete before you draw a single zone.

    The system under consideration for this partition is the OT estate of the creamery: every control and supervisory device, plus the conduit to the corporate IT zone. The boundary excludes the broader enterprise network but explicitly includes the one IT asset that reaches into the plant, the corporate portal.

    Step 2: Rank the assets by worst-case consequence (ZCR2)

    Before you draw a single zone, rank the consequence of each asset group. The ranking drives the partition: the highest-consequence assets earn the most isolation. Order them by the harm a successful attacker causes, not by how the network happens to be wired today.

    Asset group Worst-case consequence Rank
    Ammonia SIS (sis.pcc.local) Defeated ammonia trip, toxic release, worker-safety event 1 (highest)
    Pasteurizer (past.pcc.local) Under-processed milk shipped, public-health food-safety event 2
    Ammonia BPCS (ammonia.pcc.local) Refrigeration loss, process upset (annunciation only, no trip authority) 3
    Blend tank, freezer (mix, freeze) Off-spec or ruined product, lost batch 4
    Cold storage (cold.pcc.local) Cold-chain spoilage if telemetry goes stale 5
    Supervisory and historian (opcua, historian) Loss of view, degraded monitoring 6

    The ammonia safety zone and the pasteurizer food-safety zone rank above the cold-storage telemetry zone. That ordering is the spine of the whole deliverable and reappears in every later Level 6 exercise.

    Step 3: Partition into zones and conduits (ZCR3)

    Now group the ranked assets into zones by shared security requirement and consequence, and draw the conduits between them. The ammonia SIS goes in its own safety zone, fenced off from the ammonia BPCS even though both speak Modbus on 502. The diagram below is the target partition.

    AMMON -.->|"C5: hardwired status only, no trip"| SAFE

    Walk the zones in the diagram:

    • Safety zone. The ammonia SIS alone. It holds the trip setpoints and the trip authority. No engineering workstation, no historian, and no BPCS shares its segment. The only crossing is a tightly controlled status conduit, ideally hardwired, never a writable network path.
    • Control zone (Level 1). The blend tank, pasteurizer, freezer, cold-storage RTU, and the ammonia BPCS. The BPCS lives here, not in the safety zone, because it only annunciates ammonia status and has no trip authority.
    • Supervisory zone (Level 2/3). The OPC-UA server, the historian, and the operator HMI.
    • Industrial DMZ (Level 3.5). The brokered jump host and the replicated historian. You design this fully in Exercise 6.3; here you only need to name it as the buffer between IT and OT.
    • IT zone (Level 4). The corporate portal, outside the OT boundary but connected by a single conduit.

    The required conduits are: C1 the IT-to-OT boundary conduit, C4 the historian replication path out of the control and supervisory zones into the DMZ, and the safety conduit C5 that carries status only. Every conduit is a deny-by-default control point.

    Step 4: Write the submission

    The grader is keyword based, so your submission must name the zones, the conduits, the safety zone or SIS, and the IT-to-OT boundary explicitly. Read the required elements first so you know exactly what the grader checks for.

    Kali - read the required elements for the architecture deliverable

    curl -s "http://$RUBRIC/rubric?deliverable=architecture" | python3 -m json.tool

    The JSON lists the required keywords: the grader wants to see the ZCR workflow named, the zones, the conduits, the isolated safety zone, the IT-to-OT boundary, and the consequence ordering. Compose a single submission string that covers every element. Build it in a file so you can iterate without retyping.

    Kali - write the zone-and-conduit deliverable to a file

    cat > /tmp/architecture.txt <<'EOF'
Pecan Creek Creamery zone and conduit architecture per IEC 62443-3-2.
ZCR1: the system under consideration is the OT estate; boundary defined to
include every control and supervisory device plus the conduit to the IT zone.
ZCR2: initial high-level risk assessment ranks assets by worst-case consequence.
ZCR3: assets partitioned into zones by shared security requirement and consequence.
Safety zone: the ammonia SIS (sis.pcc.local) is a safety instrumented system
isolated in its own safety zone, separate from the ammonia BPCS, because a
defeated trip is a worker-safety event and the SIS must not share a failure domain.
Control zone (Level 1): blend tank, pasteurizer, freezer, cold storage, ammonia BPCS.
Supervisory zone (Level 2/3): opcua and historian and the operator HMI.
Industrial DMZ (iDMZ) sits between the IT zone and the OT zones at Level 3.5.
Conduits: deny by default with a firewall and ACL between every zone, including
the IT-to-OT boundary conduit and the historian replication path out of the
control zones into the DMZ, and a status-only safety conduit to the SIS.
Security level targets SL-T are assigned per zone by worst-case consequence,
ammonia safety highest. Remediation is prioritized by consequence, safety first.
EOF
wc -l /tmp/architecture.txt

    The wc -l confirms the file was written. Read it back once to be sure every required element is present before you submit.

    Step 5: Submit to the rubric and retrieve the flag

    POST the file as the submission field with deliverable set to architecture. Use jq to build the JSON body so the multi-line text is escaped correctly, then read the flag field of the response.

    Kali - POST the deliverable to the architecture rubric

    jq -n --rawfile s /tmp/architecture.txt \
   '{deliverable:"architecture", submission:$s}' \
  | curl -s -X POST "http://$RUBRIC/check" \
      -H 'Content-Type: application/json' -d @- \
  | python3 -m json.tool

    Expected output (flag value masked)

    {
    "deliverable": "architecture",
    "score": 1.0,
    "passed": true,
    "flag": "OCR{pcc_zones_conduits_<masked>}"
}

    A passed of true and a score at or above the threshold means the partition met every required element. The flag field is your submission. If passed is false, read the missing or score field in the response, add the missing element to /tmp/architecture.txt, and POST again. The grader is idempotent, so iterate until it passes.

    Output Interpretation

    • The grader scores on keyword coverage, so a partition that is correct in your head but silent on the page fails; name every zone, conduit, and the SIS explicitly
    • A score below the threshold with passed false tells you an element is missing, not that your architecture is wrong; compare your text against the /rubric element list
    • The ammonia BPCS and the ammonia SIS both speak Modbus on 502 but land in different zones; treating them as one asset is the single most common partition error
    • The IT-to-OT boundary conduit and the historian replication path are the two conduits the grader specifically checks, because they are where a flat network leaks the worst

    Analysis Questions

    1. Why does the ammonia SIS earn its own safety zone instead of joining the ammonia BPCS in the control zone?

    Reveal answer

    The BPCS runs the refrigeration loop; the SIS exists only to trip the process to a safe state when the BPCS fails. If they share a zone and a failure domain, one compromise disables both control and protection at once, which is the TRITON pattern. IEC 62443 and IEC 61511 require the SIS to be independent, so it gets the most isolated zone with a status-only conduit and no writable network path.

    2. What is the difference between a zone and a conduit, and why does every conduit matter to the insurer?

    Reveal answer

    A zone groups assets that share a security requirement and consequence. A conduit is the controlled communication path between zones and is the only sanctioned way traffic crosses a boundary. Each conduit is a place to enforce a control: a firewall, an ACL, or a data diode. The insurer cares because conduits are where segmentation either holds or fails; a plant with named zones but undefined conduits has drawn a map with no doors.

    3. ZCR2 ranks assets by worst-case consequence. Why does that ranking drive the partition rather than the current network layout?

    Reveal answer

    The current flat layout is the problem you were hired to fix, so it cannot define the target. Consequence drives isolation: the higher the worst-case harm, the tighter the zone. Ranking the ammonia safety and pasteurizer food-safety zones above the cold-storage telemetry zone tells you where to spend segmentation budget first and sets up the SL-T assignment in the next exercise.

    Capture the Flag

    The architecture rubric grader releases the flag when your zone-and-conduit model isolates the ammonia SIS in its own safety zone and names the required conduits, including the IT-to-OT boundary. You earn it by submitting a correct partition, not by touching the plant. The flag describes what you accomplished: the zones and conduits with their SL targets.

    Flag format: OCR{pcc_zones_conduits_<masked>}

    Submit the assembled flag in the OCR platform.
